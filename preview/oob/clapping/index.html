Why does a whole audience stop clapping at once?

The short answer

Applause spreads through a crowd like a contagion: people start clapping because the people around them are clapping. Stopping works the same way, which is why a whole audience falls silent together with no signal.

When humans approve of something, they hit their own hands together, in a group, and keep going. Nobody is in charge of it. Nobody announces the end.

Researchers who filmed audiences found that the pull to start clapping grows with the share of the room already clapping, and the pull to stop grows with the share that has already stopped. In their data, how long the applause lasted had little to do with how good the talk was. It depended on the crowd.

So an audience does not decide to stop. One or two people stop, the people who notice them stop, and the silence spreads the same way the noise did.

Sources

  1. Mann, Faria, Sumpter & Krause (2013), "The dynamics of audience applause", Journal of the Royal Society Interface 10: 20130466. Link

Questions people ask

Does longer applause mean a better performance?

Not reliably. In the 2013 study, the length of applause was driven by how clapping spread through that particular crowd, not by the quality of what they had watched.

Who decides when applause ends?

Nobody. A few people stop, the people near them notice and stop too, and the quiet spreads until the room is silent.
