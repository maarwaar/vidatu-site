Why do humans like spicy food?

The short answer

Psychologist Paul Rozin, who has studied the question since 1980, calls it "benign masochism": enjoying a burn that the body treats as danger, because the mind knows it is safe. He estimates that well over two billion adults enjoy the burn of chilli.

Chilli peppers make capsaicin, the chemical behind the burn. Mammals feel it; birds do not, and birds spread the seeds. For a mammal, the burn says: do not eat me.

Humans grow chillies on purpose and put them in their food. Most people dislike the burn at first and come to like it by eating it again and again, which is the change Rozin and Schiller studied in 1980.

One caution: humans are not the only mammal that eats them. Chinese tree shrews eat capsaicin-rich plants, because a genetic change dulls the burn for them.

Questions people ask

Why do birds not feel chilli heat?

Their receptor for capsaicin does not respond to it the way a mammal’s does, so birds eat chillies and spread the seeds.

Is liking spicy food learned?

Mostly. Rozin’s research found the liking grows with repeated eating, often from childhood, rather than being there from birth.
